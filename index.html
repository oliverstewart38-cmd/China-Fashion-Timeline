Chinese Dress Through Time

Follow four dynasties of clothing and see how politics, trade and conquest reshaped what people wore.

Tang China and Japan

Tang culture reached well beyond China. Pilgrims such as Ennin travelled from Japan in 838, and Japan adopted parts of the Chinese writing system, political structure and Confucian values. The Heian period (794–1192) began while the Tang was still in power. During it, Japan's most iconic garment, the kimono, was introduced, and women of the Imperial Court sometimes wore the junihitoe, an ensemble of 12 or more layers weighing up to 20 kg. Japan looked abroad for fashion again after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, when government officials and their wives were required to wear Western-style dress at work and on formal occasions.

What came next

The Qing dress code, which marked rank, was strictly followed until the government was overthrown in 1911. By the 1920s, Chinese elements had become very popular in the West and reached a peak. The qipao, also called the cheongsam, appeared in a more modern, fitted shape that a Chinese or Western woman could wear, and Chinese textiles and embroidery were used in Western-cut dresses. The same exchange was happening in Japan, where the West and the East began to share trends and, by the end of the 1920s, popular women's fashion had become similar across the globe, favouring a glamorous slim line.
